# Chapter 6 — Light in the Dark

*from **A Nascent Light** — Vol. 1: First Light*

---

The third room was a waterfall.

I need you to understand that sentence before I ruin it: a waterfall, *inside a king's howe*, under a hill — a seam in the cut stone at the back of the chamber with white water coming through it and gone through, an engineered thing, a *plumbing*, with a small rocky bridge going past a large hole right before it, and some light descending down from a shaft far above. The builders had not wanted their dead thirsty and had not wanted them quiet either; moving water in a tomb is a lock of sound, the way a dog is a lock of teeth. Something down here could move without anyone hearing it, or everything down here could hear them coming, which was the same tool held by the handle or by the blade.

I've been through enough of these places in books to recognize the layout even with Real Life Graphics, and that thought — *books* — was the first thing in weeks that made me feel something like home. And then the second thing that made me feel something was hanging off the bridge: a boot, and a leg, and the man the leg belonged to, facedown on a ledge three feet out from the wall, in clothes from neither this valley nor any delving I could name: fine wool, ruined; a satchel, still buckled; a hand, still reaching up, like he'd been trying to get *back* to the hole in the ceiling when gravity stopped being his employer.

"Their sniper," Father said, flatly, looking at the same hand. "They set one up top, at the opening, to shoot down at whatever comes out. And he slipped."

"He was climbing *up*, Halvard," the miller's journeyman said, in the voice of a man who has looked at the scuff pattern and wishes he hadn't. "Look at his knees. He wasn't posted. He was running."

They lowered him on his own satchel-strap, because that is what a village does with dead men even when the dead men are grave-robbers, and while the party did the work of grief and rope I stood at the far end of the room where the water noise thinned out, and looked up through the hole in the roof of the world, and did not feel much of anything, and filed that in the ledger, under *the calm*, next to the axe.

The satchel went to the baron's eyes, per Father's order — everything of theirs, opened here, reported, nothing pocketed. That was when the shaft-water had to be crossed and the bridge tested and the next door found, and that was when we met the thing the robbers had come here to be afraid of, or had fled from, or had been too busy dying to finish: the cave-bear that had made the hall of roots its last parlor.

It was massive, clearly over three meters standing, with forearms the thickness of my whole body — except that it wasn't standing, was never going to again; it lay on its side in the root-choked dark with the snow-drip of its own breath gone, its skull opened like a nut, and the blood under it was wet. Recent. Maybe a little while before the *robbers* finished dying.

Hn?

I hadn't done anything. The skill — I hadn't *cast* anything; the dead thing was simply in range of whatever the Prism's back pocket was, and the window said what it said with the placid tone of a mill accepting grain: the harvest is the harvest, host; the howe has been *gathering*, and you are the bucket. I did the math of that line at the speed of a boy standing next to a fresh corpse: it meant a dead man had come down here with a lamp and a bad conscience and a skill like mine — *like mine* — and the howe had collected him the way it collected everything else. It meant "harvest" wasn't about killing at all. It meant the rule I'd been telling myself — *only what I end, only what I've earned* — was a courtesy I had invented, and the system had *accepted the terms politely*, the way a creditor accepts a partial payment and keeps the note.

Then we found the draugr.

Not a lot of them. That's the part the ballads get wrong about howes: they promise an *army*, because armies are free in a story; what a howe actually keeps is a *staff* — a door-guard here, raised from his niche with blue flame filling empty eye-sockets and flicking toward us as he slowly rose, so slow, so utterly without hurry, that Ove got a stone in his face before the thing had finished the decision to stand; a "strong one" in the hall with the roots, heavy armor gone to rust bloom, a war-hammer on his back, who turned its head at the sound of the lamp the way a sleeper turns toward a clock; and then — and then, past the fifth door, past the hall where two of the robbers had ended up with their lamps guttered out beside them, the drag-marks in the silt telling you which way this howe liked to eat, the *master of the crypt*, in a room built like a held breath.

He stood in a coffin-sized niche that had once had a lid, and he was *not* moving, and that was worse. Blue flame behind the eye-sockets, steady as two candles in a church. On his breast, rusted to the shape of what it had been, a sun-and-spear plate, and in his hands, folded across him like he was buried with the manners of a lord, a sword the length of my thigh, *edge bright*.

Everything in me went flat and professional. The Prism's calm came up like a tide being *ordered*.

"He's got an old word for *die*," the miller's journeyman said, and put his axe into his hands with the shaking care of a man doing a thing he had seen done. "That's the warning, men. Whatever's behind him, he's paid for it in *voice*."

What happened next is a sequence I have checked against memory forty times, and it goes exactly like this: the door-guard came up off its niche *behind* us, and the strong one in the hall started walking with the unhurried arithmetic of rust and old grudges, and the master *spoke*, and Runa's warning arrived three weeks too late and one second too early.

The word — because that's all it was, a *single word* with too many consonants in it — came into the room through my ears and my teeth and the marrow of my shins, and it said to my body, *stop*, and the reason I did not, I will now put in writing for the first time, is that my body has a landlord I answer to and the landlord put a filter on it: the Word of Binding arrived at me the way everything else arrived at me lately, *processed* — fear logged, volume down, meaning intact and *harmless*, like a shout heard through a waterfall. Around me the party went to the ground or to their knees; the smith's boy simply *folded*, and his lamp went out, and the dark took the room by the throat.

I did not.

I walked. That's all I had, in that moment: I walked in front of the master of the crypt, in the black, by the light of two blue flames, with six Motes in my account and the shard humming through the far door like a plucked wire, and the hum was an *arithmetic*, and I was on the wrong side of the equals sign — so I spent three of them right there, standing in the water-dark like a man buying bread in a burning city:

I threw it at his face.

It is a small mercy that the master of the crypt had not been in a position to have his sense of humor re-evaluated in eleven hundred years, because the flash *worked*: not on him, on his *aim*, on the half-second of a dead man's instinct that made him bring a forearm up across his own eyes — an eye-shield is a living habit, and habits are older than courage, even the borrowed kind — and in that half-second I was inside the reach of the sword and *behind* the lid-stone the robbers had moved, with a crowbar's hole to the next chamber and my rope at my hip, and I did the only thing a level-four shepherd with three Motes still unspent and a landlord of a system could do:

I got the master's attention off the party, and I *kept* it.

"Here!" I shouted into the dark, in the way of a boy throwing himself at exactly nothing and hoping the debt collects on him instead of the men. "Here, you *ledger*—"

He turned. The blue flames found me. The Word came again — it came *again*, of course it did, they're built to spend them — and this time, this time the filter caught it the way a roof catches rain: *Prism Calm engaged*, and the room went quiet in me, and something else went *bright*: a single line of type at the bottom of my vision, in ink that had never been in a menu before, in a voice that was the Attendant's own, and it said:

[Good. Hold him two breaths.]

I held him three. What happened in the third breath, the party's later testimony is not consistent about — the master of the crypt went *still*, still beyond dead, with his head turned toward the niche at the back of the room, where the robbers' lid lay pushed aside and where, in the box of a king, something had begun to *shine*.

The light in that tomb was not ours, and the draugr loved it the way a docked dog loves a master who has come back with a whip: he *went* to it, past me, past the journeyman's axe which rang off his rusted breast like a dropped spoon, and the whole room went to it with him — every lamp, every shadow, the fall of the water beyond the bridge — because the *shard* in the king's box had woken, and I could feel it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in a dark room: my shoulder, mine, *summoned*.

The walk to the box took four steps, and I counted them the way I count everything, and at the third I understood, with the calm's own cold clarity, that the whole howe was plumbing in *two* directions. Water to keep the dead from thirst. A door to keep the living honest. And behind the fifth, a piece of a broken god's eye waiting for — and I use this phrasing with full knowledge of the size of myself — a *hand that could read it*.

I picked it up.

I want to note, for the record and for whoever inherits this account, that every rational part of me had voted *no*. The shard was the size of a hen's egg, a chip of something like glass and not glass, all angles and no seam, and it had *edges of light* along the angles, and when I closed my hand on it the sound in the howe went off like a bell pulled out of water.

I ran.

The exit was not the way in — the way in was full of a master of the crypt being *thorough* with the men on the floor — so I did the other thing: I took the shaft-water head-on, the bridge, the ledge, the wall, with the water's *noise* coming down at me, because if the tomb's lock of sound was water, water was the way out, and the second the blue flames got my back the howe itself finished the argument — a crack, from the niche where the lid lay shifted, long and dry and *decisive*, and then another, and the whole third room began to take its ceiling *back* — the builders' flood-door, Runa's ledger in another tense: *a howe that pays you back exactly is a howe that's keeping score*, and it had just found a debt worth collapsing a hill for.

Father got the rope at the satchel-strap handhold with one hand and got me under the arms with the other and hauled, and the party *moved* in the way men move when they have been shamed out of dying in a hall with their *children* watching — and I want to put this in the record too: I looked back, at the bridge, in the falling dark, and the master stood in the water with his head up and his face *unreadable*, and did not follow, and my blood ran at a temperature that had nothing to do with the snow, because a dead man's face can be unreadable in two ways and the second is that he has *somewhere else to be*.

We came out into the first thin light at noon, six men and one boy, and the smith's other boy carried — ankle gone, alive, which we said *aloud* eleven times on the walk down before we believed it — and the north face closed behind us with a sound like a book, and the snow settled on the breach, and in my fist, closed around something no baron's writ could itemize, the light pulsed slow as a second heartbeat, learning my name *again*, from the inside.

I sat down in the snow next to the four pairs of neat boots, and my father stood over me, holding his own blood off his sleeve with his thumb, and he looked at my fist, and he looked at my face, and he asked me, in the tone other fathers use for *what have you done*, the one thing that mattered:

"What did you *touch*."

And I looked up at him with the snow going grey in my hair, and I did the arithmetic of my whole life, old and new, in one column, and I said the truest sentence I could afford:

"I touched a debt," I said. "Yours now, too. I'm sorry. I'd do it again."

He hit me with the back of his hand again, hard enough to ring, and then he wrapped both his arms around the top of my head like I was eight, like I was *Astrid*, and I heard him tell the party, in the voice of a man posting a notice: "He was under the bridge when the roof went. That's the story, that's the report, that's what the bailiff gets. Any man of you sells the boy's *light* to a crown, you come home to no pen and no pelt and no welcome, in that order."

"Yes sir," said the party, all of them, including the wounded one, with the unanimity of men who had decided, in a hall, to be a village instead.
