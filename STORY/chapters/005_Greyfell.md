# Chapter 5 — Greyfell

*from **A Nascent Light** — Vol. 1: First Light*

---

The tracks got us a visit instead of a story, which is how you know the difference between a village and a kingdom: a village sends an old woman with soup, and a kingdom sends a man with a folder.

What the tracks said, translated from the snow and my own level-four eyes, was simple: something had driven the wolves down off the high timber, and had then gone, unhurried, into the one place in the valley with a door that no one could explain. Two weeks of that question being asked in every kitchen from the Ford to the ford — and then Bailiff Soren Kel rode in from Halloway with a factor on his left and a scrivener on his right, the baron's arithmetic made flesh, and he did not come to answer the question. He came to *bill* it.

We were at the sheep shed when the riding came up, Father and me and Ove the smith's boy — Ove had lost a bet involving a gate and a goat, and had spent four days repaying it with honest labor under the supervision of my sister, who had appointed herself the foreman of it. Kel reined up at the pen wall, looked at the braced boards and the two hung pelts, and ran his mouth before his manners caught up with it, the way the low-born notice about the gentry's opinions.

"Your boy killed a wolf," he said, to Father, as if it were a tax item. "Mangy, by the pelt. Good."

"He did," my father said, and the two words were a door closing so politely you could hear the bolt from the road.

"Greyfell's been opened," Kel said. "Not by wolves. The survey the last baron paid for — before his *son's* time, before the mound was on anyone's books but the church's — has come up out of the dust, and it is a curious thing, but the old writ reads howes, and howes read as mineral and monument: the crown's, the baron's by gift of the crown, and the March's by the baron's sufferance. Which means—" he spread the folder like a man laying down cards, "—that the matter of whether your village is *negligent* regarding the lord's monument, and the matter of whether the baron's grace will *waive* the inquest fee for a season, are the same matter, and both can be settled by a small service."

Father did not look at me when he said it. "Speak plain or speak once, Bailiff."

"Plain, then. Someone has broken ground at the howe's north face. The baron will know what was taken, or he will know who took it, and he would prefer to know at your expense rather than his. He asks for a survey: a party from the village, with light and line, to make the baron's eye down the opened work and report. In exchange, the season's inquest is waived, the fee forgiven, and — this is my own clause, Vester, you'll note the *hand* on the parchment — the village keeps the right of first refusal on any delving the baron later licenses."

"What's the fee," Father said, "if you don't."

"Nine marks," said the scrivener, from behind his sleeve.

I felt my father do the sum beside me, and the yard do the sum with him, and the arithmetic of a good hand go red around the edges: nine marks was the year's slack. Nine marks was Astrid's shoes and the roof's new half. It was the price of a question somebody else opened.

"We'll have an answer by dark," Father said, to the sheep, and Kel took it for an answer to him, and the visit was over, and the neighborhood came, which is the other thing a kingdom sends: nothing, and neighbors, which is the same thing at higher interest.

***

The party, when it was made up, was six men and one errand-runner, and the errand-runner was me, which I had not asked for, and which my mother had *prohibited*, in the kitchen, with the bowl she was drying held at a height that made it a fact rather than a suggestion.

"Signe," my father said, in the tone he used on the one fence in the township that argued. "He found the gate before I did. He braced it right. He's read the baron's writs out loud twice now, and Kel *listened* to him. If there's old writing down there, he'll know what it is before the smith starts selling it back to us as luck. And if there's a fine sitting on our boards because my son wanted—"

"I know what it's sitting on," she said.

"I know that you know." The door-quiet of them was its own kind of shouting; I was in the doorway with a bucket, doing that thing where adults pretend to have forgotten you exist, which is how you hear everything that matters. "He'll go with the rope on the same side as me, and he comes home, or none of them come home, and the baron can have the sheep and the howe and the *reputation*, in that order."

"Then you'll want the good rope," my mother said, and went to the press where the good rope was.

Which is how, two days later, I found myself at the base of the cold hill at the first thin hour, in a line of men who talked about the weather the way the weather talks about us, with Ove and his goat-debt, the smith's other boy, the miller's journeyman, two shepherds whose names the story does not require, and my father, who had stopped three paces below the mound and was not climbing further, because Runa had gotten to him first.

The old woman had come out of the house on her own feet, and she stood in the snow with her shawl over everything she owned, and she said — not loudly, and not to all of us; to my father, which is how a story gets into a family:

"You'll keep the boy's eyes on the *steps*, Halfdan. Where a man looks at the walls of a howe is what wakes it. My grandmother's work was the north door, and her grandmother's the king's own barrow, and neither of them found a thing, and both of them came back with the same coin in their hands that they'd left with, *and no more* — do you understand me? A howe that pays you back exactly is a howe that's keeping score. The door with no door has a keeper, and keepers don't retire. Word of warning from an old woman who's nothing to you: whatever's down there very likely knows how to *use its voice*, and the light it likes is not ours. Turn back at the third room, or don't turn at all, but decide *now*, because down there is too late."

Father looked at her for a long moment, and then he looked up at the mound, and then at me, and I could see the man deciding which parts of the old woman to keep. Then he reached down, took the spare coil off his own pack, and roped himself to the smith's boy, and then roped himself to me, and then went up the steps without another word, which is the most honest sermon our valley exports.

And I want to say here, so the record of it doesn't get *improved*: I wasn't afraid. The Prism's calm was up at the back of my skull, and I could feel it working on the fear the way the mill works on the grain — taking it out of me in even, measured portions, giving me back a boy standing on the snow at the foot of a dead king's doorknob, *efficient*. What I felt instead was a small, flat, professional curiosity, and beneath that a line of the ledger reading itself: *the dark they ran from has teeth; the howe has had its teeth in it for two weeks; we are arriving late to someone else's meal*. Curiosity is fine, the old books will tell you. Curiosity is how you find things. Curiosity is not how you survive them; what survives them is the man who decided at the foot of the steps to turn back at the third room.

I made that decision now, for later, in writing, because down there is too late.

"Thanks," I muttered, and the quest line blinked, satisfied, the way an app does when it has decided you're fine.

The breach was on the north face, exactly where the survey said it would be, and exactly where Runa had said to look away, which is the trouble with old women: they're right in the wrong tense. Someone had been there before us — a camp, or the remains of a camp, turned-over logs, a clear fire-ring with cold ash and a scatter of provisions lying about where the people who'd owned them had stopped wanting them.

Father counted the packs at a glance and disliked his arithmetic so much he said nothing for ten full seconds, which is roughly what ten seconds of my father saying nothing costs in the local market.

"That's four bed-rolls," the miller's journeyman said, in the voice of a man volunteering to be wrong. "And the pots. Who eats and doesn't boil, I ask you—"

"Nobody's gone off without their boots," one of the shepherds said, and it was true, and the true thing lay in the snow in a neat pair like an offering, and the snow in front of the broken ground was not, in fact, neat; the snow there had been *worked*, scuffed and trampled and —

"—that's blood," Ove said. "Fresh. The wind hasn't had time on it yet."

It had not. There was a smear of it down the broken steps where a man had been dragged, or where a man had fallen and been helped by methods I would not enjoy describing to a girl of eight, and along with the blood a dropped thing: a short iron bar with a hooked end. The prying kind. Delver's work — a crowbar made for lifting lid-stones, and it had been *dropped*, not lost, because a man drops his bar when something at the other end of the door has his full attention, and the far end of that door gaped at us with its mouth full.

The smith's boy said the word everyone came to the howe for and no one wanted to say aloud.

"Dwarven," he said, meaning *older-than-any-of-us-caring-to-say*, and he lit the first lamp, and the mound exhaled at him — a breath of dead air out of the dark, exactly at that second, exactly at that temperature, the kind of coincidence the human brain files under *warning* whether or not the brain has been formally introduced to the file.

"My *boy*," Father said, very quietly, to the sky — "the one who's been *logging* to me all week like the trees wrote back. Whatever little trick that is. *Not in here.*"

"Yes sir," I said, and I did not open the skill again, and I want the record to show that this is what the white room had been talking about when it said my patience would be *load-bearing*.

He held the rope a hand's-width from me when we went down. The third room was our line in the sand, and I walked it in my mind while he spoke it aloud to the party, and the first room was nothing — cut steps, damp, the smell of lime and old water — and the second room was a hall full of roots and a floor full of the *wrong kind* of rubble, and the third room was the door.

And the door with no door — the one in the boy's dream, the one the bailiff's factor had chalked a rubbing of nothing at all — was *standing open*.

Not broken. Not forced. Open, the way a door is open when somebody is expecting company, with a single tall lamp burning on the far side of it, steady, in still air, its flame pointed *in*, like a finger.

Father looked down at the four dead men's boots, and then up at the light, and I watched a man do the arithmetic of a nine-mark fine against the arithmetic of a room, and I watched the *first* of them win, and I said the only thing a person in my position can say:

"On the rope," I said. "All of us. And I'm in front, because I'm the lightest thing you've got and whatever's in there has *already* fought an axe."

He agreed, which is to say he hit me with the back of his hand hard enough to ring and then roped me to the front of the line, and the party passed the third room's threshold, into the light, and the cold hill closed its ledger one step at a time behind us.
