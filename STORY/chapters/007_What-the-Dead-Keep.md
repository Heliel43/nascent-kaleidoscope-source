# Chapter 7 — What the Dead Keep

*from **A Nascent Light** — Vol. 1: First Light*

---

The report the village filed was, in the main, a true one: breach by outside delvers; four dead, one within our knowledge; the work collapsed by its own aging; nothing recoverable, nothing worth the fee. Bailiff Kel read it twice in the saddle, asked one question — "and the boy?" — and rode for Halloway with his ledger balanced the way the Vester fence line had been, which is to say *by somebody else's labor, courteously acknowledged*. The nine marks stayed where they were: not owing.

I learned, that spring, the shape of the price of a lie told by good people.

The smith's boy wore his ankle in a boiled-leather cast and a permanent grievance against the word "heroics." Two of the robbers stayed in the howe with their lamps. The fourth we never named, because the drag-marks ended at the water and the water kept the receipt. And every night that week the village came to our yard in the small amounts that neighbors come: a cheese, a half-pail of kindling, the Duvens' apology in the form of a ham, and Runa last of all, who stood by the pen bracing I had laid out in the dark a lifetime ago, ran one finger down the forked rail, and said to no one:

"Aye. The boy tied the knots like a man who's afraid of the wrong things. That's the fear that builds."

My mother, meanwhile, kept her own appointment with me, the way she kept all appointments: in the woodshed, with the door open for propriety, at the hour the light went amber.

"You have been carrying something around the house like a lamp under a bushel," she said. "I have caught you three times since the howe looking at your own hands as if they were witness stands. You will tell me what it is, not because I can fix it, but because I am your mother and silence is a room I will not have you furnish alone."

What I told her was true as far as it went and incomplete in the exact places she would look for — which is how I knew, that afternoon, that I had inherited more than her midwife's hands: she had taught me *report discipline* by example for five years without once saying the words. "The howe is awake," I said. "It's not a haunted place. It's a *managed* one — doors, water, a keeper, a ledger of debts. What took me past its check was… light. Old, and broken, and *looking*. I've got part of it, under my ribs, and it is quiet, and I do not like how well I sleep with it there."

She asked four questions. Three of them were medical — the sleeping, the eating, the shaking, and did it *hurt*, and did the hurt change the way the boy moved — and the fourth was, after a long look at my face: "does it make you kinder or crueller."

"I don't know yet," I said, and the honesty of it cost me something, and she nodded like a woman closing a bill of sale, and that was the end of it, which is how the best conversations on this farm had always ended, at the point where the other person stopped expecting more.

"That's the right answer," she said. "The wrong ones are both of the others. You will come to me on the nights the light is loud, and you will not *perform* it coming, and Kale—" the midwife's face, all business, "—if you start talking to yourself again like the shed has opinions, you'll do it where I can hear. The shed will not testify for you, and I am *tired* of finding you out by my own son's manners."

"Yes ma'am," I said, and meant it with the whole of the flat, calm, *informed* place the Prism kept my feelings in, and — I noticed it even as I noticed — loved her anyway, from the other side of the filter, the way a man reads about the sea on a frozen morning: the facts arriving with the salt missing.

***

The season turned, as the valley's seasons do, by slow argument: the frost lost the high timber first, then the meadows, then the mind of the river, and by the month the delvers' road was passable, Garrow's Ford was doing what it always did — *surviving with interest* — and a company of four riders came up the track from the ford at the wrong hour of the day, which was how you knew, in a place like ours, before you saw the manes, that they were either trouble or customers, and the only honest difference was paperwork.

The Tresh crew — that name came later, out of Benn's own mouth like a brand he'd inherited — came into the village with the particular unhurriedness of men who had ridden past three signs saying *slow down* and were *not* here to be told by a goat-herd's chalk what to do. They were not a pretty company: a quartermaster's saddlebags on a hired mule, a shield with a *useful* crack in it, a mage in a coat with more pockets than the law allowed, and an outrider — slim, sun-dark, the age of the mill, who sat her horse the way a good chair sits: *indifferent to being noticed*.

They had come for four men. That was the whole of their errand, and it took the village an hour of the market's little arithmetic — the prices of oats, the *prices* of sympathy, who owed who for the lambing season and how much that debt was worth today, in cash, in information — before Benn Tresh stood in the meeting-yard with his hat in his hand, holding a list of names like a man holding a rope, and asked for the report *again*, and got it.

"Four bed-rolls," he said, when the shepherds were done. "You'll have described that in the report you sent the baron, if you're honest, and I'll wager the fine you dodged on a lie was this: you *buried* that description in the copy. Because the dead are nobody's problem until the living name them, and in this valley the living keep their ledgers to themselves, and—" he looked past the assembled village, at one farm's smoke, and then at me, standing on the outside of the crowd with my hands in my pockets because there was nowhere else to put them, "—and because one of your *boys* came back out of that howe, and I want to meet the child who did the arithmetic on a king's doorway."

So that is how the meeting went: two ledgers, read at each other, across the stumps of a broken pen wall, at the edge of the yard where my sister had, unprompted, seated herself like a bailiff's cat, holding the squirrel's acorn supply of two winters in both fists, offering none.

They camped at the ford that night, at Kel's leave, and the crew's business with the howe went the way all delving business goes here: the village held the baron's first-refusal clause; the crew held the dead men's *rights*, which in law are only as good as the living who assert them; and between the two sat the story — the shape of the rooms, the water, the third door, which had been a *good* man and which had taken a boy's life from him by inches and then the rest of it. The story was for sale. Everyone was too honest to call it that, so nobody named the price, and everyone listened to Benn Tresh, very politely, talk about *recompense for the loss of four good workmen*, while my father talked about the roof, and the smith talked about his son's ankle, and the numbers moved between them like a stone under the snow.

I did not mean to get in front of it. It happened the way an axe happens: the arithmetic of the yard's offers came out of my mouth, in full, with the columns, before the old men had finished the first draft of it —

"Their kit is yours; it never becomes the howe's. The site is the baron's; the village holds first refusal and will forfeit it quietly rather than hold it. What you're actually buying is the shape of the rooms — *that* is the only grave good that's undug — and you'll pay it in the only coin this side of the Ford: three marks to the smith for the ankle and the cast and the years of it; four to the families of the two who stay down there, paid by whoever walks out alive; and the work you do here you price at the *town* rate and not the valley rate, because if you come back — and by the look in your quartermaster's eye you'll come back twice — you'll want a village that isn't charging itself for the pleasure of your company."

Silence, of the particular kind that follows a child into a room.

Maren — that was the outrider's name; I learned it later, from Benn, the way you learn the name of a weather system — Maren Skell, who had been standing the whole time at the rail with her arms crossed and her horse's bridle looped over one forearm like a man who never once *sat down* at an obligation, looked at me over the top of that silence for a long second, and said, to nobody in particular, "Huh."

It was not a kind word. It was a *professional* one, and I have never in either life been complimented more accurately.

That was the first time. The second was at the mound, two days later, at the hour the light was going the color of weak tea, when the crew's first look at the site was scheduled and I was standing three paces below the closed breach doing the thing I did best: counting what was *missing*. She came up the steps around me without any sound she was willing to be held to account for, and stood, and looked at the scarp where the north face had knit itself shut, and said:

"The bailiff's report says nothing's in there but a cave-in."

"Yes."

"And you're standing where a delver stands," she said, "when he's decided the report is a *first draft*." She didn't turn her head. The wind moved the tails at her shoulders. "I watched you at the pen. You don't haggle like a boy and you don't flinch like a soldier, which leaves you being either a very strange child or somebody's very strange problem, and I'd rather know which one I'm standing next to."

"I checked the water table," I said, because there was no reason to lie to the only person in the crew who had asked a question instead of a price. "The breach closed outward. A cave-in closes *in*. Whatever's under there didn't collapse, it *shut the door*."

She let that sit; we let it sit together; and out of the sitting came, finally, the second thing she said to me in our acquaintance, and the reason my whole life is divided into the before and after of it:

"Then we'll need better doors than this one," she said, and looked down the slope at the village, and back at the howe, and filed both, and started walking down, and at the fourth step threw me the last fact of the morning like a coin I hadn't earned:

"Runa's right about the keeper. My grandmother was the gravedigger at Saltmarsh for forty years, and the one thing she'd say about the old king-howes, over the fire, in the voice she used when she wasn't *joking*, was: *the keeper doesn't guard the gold — the gold guards the keeper.* Whatever your 'light' is, stranger-Boy — it's not in the howe to be *found*. It's in the way, to keep us *out*. You understand me?"

"Yes," I said, in a voice that had gone, in one syllable, down to the basement of me.

"Good," she said, and then, because the wind and the light were right and she was, at sixteen, still a person who said the true thing and fled before it could be *held*: "Don't come up the mound alone again. It's bad arithmetic, and the howe keeps score, and—" already down the slope, not turning, hand flat on the saddle horn, "—*we* keep score too. Tell Benn I'll need your rope-line for the trial dig. That's not me being kind, that's me being paid by the day and needing the rope."

"'Water?'" I called after her, at random, at the back of her head, like an idiot, like a child — the test-word, my own, from a night and a pen and an axe, spoken now to a girl who had never heard it.

She stopped mid-stride. Turned one shoulder.

"'Water,'" she said, and I have been many things in two lives and the list is *long*, and I can tell you precisely what it is to be *answered* in a language you invented for yourself, at the wrong time, by a person who heard all of it: my mother's whole vocabulary of the face went over hers in the space of one breath — and she did not soften, exactly, she was not built out of that; she *acknowledged*, the way you acknowledge a country on a map, and mounted, and was gone down the steps.

*Yeah,* I said to the corner of my vision. *I know.*

The satchel they buried came up out of the baron's strongbox by right of the next morning: the dead climber's, fine wool rotted to a memory, satchel-buckle green. It held chalk, rubbing-paper, two days' hard bread, a bill of a Saltmarsh factor's on twelve hundred marks' credit — and the journal.

Benn held the book in his hands the way you hold a bird with a broken wing, and did not open it, in front of us. His brother's hand was on that clasp; he knew it the way the crew had known the boot sizes in the report. He closed the lid on the whole thing, turned it in his palm once, and said to the village, in the flat, level voice of a quartermaster making sure the record was clean, "It's sealed. Not by him."

And it was — that was the reason the book had *survived* the damp forty years' worth of howe-water — a band of some pale metal about the covers, and a seal at its clasp like a wax drop grown into a shape: an eye, shut.

Cato looked at it the way a man looks at the road in front of him when a horse has shied, and the smile he kept on his face for the village was the kind you wear when you want the children to hear adult conversation in peace, and then the old men went to haggle about oats and the hedge-mage stood in the shadow of our smoke-hole and took off his hat and said, to the four of us who remained — Benn, Maren, the shieldman Brant, and me, because nobody had thought to send away the errand-runner who *talked like a ledger* — said, quietly, in the voice Runa had used at the mound and nobody had believed:

"I was an apprentice in the Tower of Saltmarsh before I was a thief," Cato said. "Fifty years, of which eleven were spent *filing* for a house across the water that never laughed, never drank, and never opened anything. The Closed Eye was the surveying arm of the Order of the Last Account. This seal on this journal was set on it *from the outside*, by their field-office, to keep a *man* out of his own thoughts." He looked at the howe's sealed mouth, above us, over the village's roofs, and put his hat back on like a man closing a door in a house with someone awake upstairs.

"I had very much hoped they were a tax write-off by now," he said.

Above the yard, my level sat in its corner of the world at a stolid **5**, and my Motes at **6**, and the light under my ribs pulsed once — slow, unbothered, the tempo of a second heartbeat learning the tempo of a house — like a hand laid flat over a mouth.

The howe kept score.

And now, it turned out, so did somebody else.
