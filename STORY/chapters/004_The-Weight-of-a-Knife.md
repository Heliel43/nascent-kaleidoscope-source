# Chapter 4 — The Weight of a Knife

*from **A Nascent Light** — Vol. 1: First Light*

---

Here's the thing about a system that grades your life: it turns out a boy can level up just by doing the work he was doing anyway, as long as some part of him is *counting*.

In the six weeks after the hare, the Prism fed me XP like a bookkeeping program feeds a spreadsheet — small, constant, earned. **[Woodsman (Common) Lv.3 — +1 DEX]**, for the cordwood I stacked by the light of my own stubbornness until my hands stopped blistering and started callousing in the correct places. **[Herb-lore (Common) Lv.2]**, for learning, at Mother's elbow, which green things the howe folk paid for and which ones the howe folk did not. **[Spears (Uncommon) Lv.1]**, a class of XP that shouldn't have existed on a farm and that I had to explain away to myself as *poles for the sheep pen, and the pen needed new poles anyway*.

Level 3 arrived on a morning of frost and fish-gutting and made me feel, absurdly, like a man who'd finally paid off a carriage. I spent the three points the ledger way: VIT first, always the house; DEX second, because I would fight with hands, not with hope. Motes I did not spend. I had harvested three more — a pine marten in a trap, a trout big as my forearm, an owl with a broken wing I'd found in the snow and ended myself, because Mote Sense was right, that one: I had *felt* it about to stop, across a whole field, like a candle going out in a house down the road, and I had walked faster than I needed to.

Motes: 3. I saved them the way Father saved the good nails.

I want to tell you I was ready. What I was, was *informed*, which in a frontier autumn passes for ready the way a full pantry passes for a feast. What we had was an outhouse, two hundred sheep, and the arithmetic of a week: the bailiff's factor wanted the last of the ash rails by the feast day, so Father went to the Ketterings' place to trade a day's work for a team — their ewes were lambing wrong and their hired man was more wrong — and Mother rode with him, because the Ketterings' girl was due any day and Mother's calendar said otherwise, and Mother's calendar was the only church our valley tithed to.

They left at first light with the horse and the bad weather in their faces. Astrid cried about it, which was a fair ruling on the situation by anyone's standards.

"Two days," Father said, swinging up. "You've got the tall boy's head on your shoulders, Kale. Pen gate, night latch, and no stories." He looked at me one beat longer than the words needed. He always had, since the cup of ice. He'd never asked, which was either trust or the frontier version of *not yet*.

"Hearth's mine," I said. "Latch is mine. Stories are Astrid's, she's better at them."

And that was the whole of the briefing, and I want it said plainly, later, when the story gets told and someone insists on finding the omen: there was no omen. There was only the week, and the rain, and the fact that winter had come at the howe early and thin, and wolves that had been eating badly for a month had decided the sheep were a nearer dinner than the deer.

Harsh is the realization that any animal could turn on you.

I'd had it explained to me my whole life by people who made a living off animals, but understanding a thing and *believing* it are two different buildings, and on the night it came, I moved between them like a man crossing a courtyard in rain.

I heard the dogs first — and we didn't have dogs, which is why I heard them: the yard-dogs of the place a quarter-mile down, the Duvens', barking in the ragged, stopping, restarting rhythm that means *something is wrong and we are not equipped*, and then the silence after that, which means the same thing in a worse tense.

Then the sheep went up.

You have never heard two hundred sheep decide to be frightened until you've heard it through the floor of a house built on a slope, and I can promise you that every story I ever loved about such a sound had lied about the music in it.

"Kale." Astrid was already upright, already the size of herself in the doorway. I had thought through the fight before I thought *fight*: this is what being old inside, whatever the ledger called it, was actually for.

"Axe off the nail. Fire-pan off the hook. Go *down* the ladder, not the stairs — the stairs' sound carries to the pen wall and they'll trample the gate trying to meet you. Get in the shed, take the ladder to the pen, get inside and *do not come out*, and when I say through the boards, you say 'water'."

"'Water?'"

"So I know you said it and not the wind. *Go.*"

I have never in two lives moved that fast on that little. The pen wall was two courses of timber and one of brush-weave; the gate was hazel and a withy latch — good work, quick work, the sort that keeps wolves out forty-one nights a year and fails on the forty-second.

I got the north side of it done — that is, I got one board off and one board on, bracing the latch side with a forked rail — when the barking died and the sheep died, and the silence that filled the yard was the silence of animals doing exactly what I had told Astrid not to do. They were *facing the gate*. Two hundred wool-backed heads, and nothing behind them moving at all.

Wolves don't come into the light unless the dark they're running from has teeth. Runa said it that very week over a bowl of something that could've used more salt, and I had it coming back now, unbidden, the way a folk proverb gets into the water supply of your thinking and comes out of you at the worst possible moment, which is the only moment proverbs ever get used.

*The dark has teeth.* Fine. The dark could take its opinions and file them.

The gap I hadn't braced — I hadn't braced it, I had *laid out* the bracing and let myself get talked out of it by the third wolf shadow, a lean one, watching like an auditor, which told me the first two were the muscle, so the plan was a gate rush and a corner kill — the gap went the way of the latch, with a sound like the door of a tavern giving up entirely on a bad Tuesday.

I took my stand in the yard with the fire-pan between us, because a wolf's arithmetic of a man is fire plus stick plus *size*, and I was short on the last, so I stacked the first two in front of me like a ledger with a point to make.

The first wolf came in low and fast, a mangy grey thing with a winter's hunger in its eyes. I did the right thing and it was like being right for the first time in eleven years of practice. I *moved*, and the axe met it where it was going to be. There's an economy to a farm axe — you don't swing at a wolf, you set the angle and let the running do the hauling — and the edge found shoulder, and it took, and the pain arrived at the animal's end a half-second late, like the world's mail being slow, and then the noise it made — not a howl, a *curtain being torn* — and then blood, hot across my face, and it was over, and I stood in the middle of my yard with my heart beating in a steady rhythm because *that* was the part that frightened me the most.

Logged. It *logged* it. I could feel the fear as a fact, the way you read about a fire — three hundred and twelve degrees of the body, the chemicals all correct, the data all present — and none of it with any volume, and I was running, and I was *efficient*, and the second wolf came round the fire-pan's blind side exactly as the third had intended and I gave it the flat of the axe through the nose because killing had a cooldown on my stomach I hadn't consented to, and it went down screaming, and the auditor — the lean one, the plan of them — took one more look at the geometry of the yard, at the boy standing in the middle of it not shaking the way things in stories shake, and that was when I understood, cold in a way the frost in my hand had never been, what the Prism was actually for.

The wolves had been hunting *badly* for a month. Wolves that thin are bold with sheep and careful with *people*, and here they'd come two-and-one at a gate I'd had a day to fix. Starving, yes. But not stupid. Something had taken their good ground and left them the valley's leftovers, and it had left them *nervous* enough to gamble, and animals that gamble lose to a fire-pan and a mad axe at midnight, and — and —

I want the record to show I did not decide to throw it. The decision was made by the part of me that had planned the fence line, and the lance took the second wolf across the ribs in a crackle of blue-white and it *broke stride* — didn't fall, didn't die; the limb went dead under it and it hit the boards and started dragging itself with its nose pointed at the gate, and I finished it because finishing it was in the plan, and I have written down, in the ledger, on the page about what the calm takes, that I felt every stroke of the axe the same amount and *cared* about half of it.

The auditor fled into the dark, and I stood over the two dead, in my yard, in the stink of blood and sheep, at the end of my second life, level four and two Motes richer, and I said out loud, to the trees, to the house, to nobody, in the voice of a person for whom the price of competence had just been fully itemized:

"…I should have braced the north board."

The trees did not answer. This is one of the great services the trees perform for us.

Then the door of the shed opened, because of course it opened, because Astrid was my sister, because she had heard everything through nine inches of boards and one act of parliament, and she came down the ladder in her night-shift and stood in the light of the overturned fire-pan with the two dead wolves ten paces off, and she looked at them, and she looked at me, and she said, in the steady, reasonable voice of the eight-year-old: "Did you *kill* them," and I said, "Yes," and then she started crying, and I found I could not do it with her, and I stood there with the axe in my hand and my heart at the wrong pace, doing arithmetic about the pen gate, doing it in front of my crying sister, because it was the only volume I had left, and that, too, went on the page.

She stopped because I knelt, and knelt with the blood still on me, and said the thing that was true: "I killed them before they came near you. That's the whole sentence. You can ask it again tomorrow, when it's daylight, and I'll tell you it again, and I'll tell you whether it keeps working when you say it."

"'Kay," she said, like it was a truce and not an agreement, and then she was on me, and small people are the only ones allowed to knock a big thing like a man flat, and I let her, and I put my chin on top of her head, and I looked over her at the woods the way a person looks at a debt that has been called.

My mother came home at dawn. What she did when she saw the yard I have no memory of reporting, because I was being held from behind — both my parents, both arms around me, the smell of horse and rain on her, and she was saying things to my hair in a language I didn't speak, which everyone in our valley speaks to the sheep sometimes, and I think they mostly do it for the humans in the pen.

"Two dead and one ran off," Father said to her, in the flat tone of a man giving a statement to a magistrate. "He braced the south wall with a forked rail and put the ladder in the shed *before it happened*. He called it down, Signe. He called it in half a night. He killed a wolf with an axe and then killed another one *in order*, and then he stood in my yard and *checked the boards*." He turned me by the shoulders, and looked down at me, and the thing in his face was not gratitude and not fear and not pride, and I had to live eleven years in this family before meeting it, and I knew it from the white room like an old friend: *calculation*. "What are you?" he asked, without malice, and my mother went very still behind me, and over the top of his shoulder I could see the far line of the snow, the white fields that ran up to the cold hill, and across them, plain as a signature, running, running, running — a set of tracks from the woods, long-strided, unhurried, and none of them going *toward* the wolves' den.

All of them going toward the howe.

"You're shaking now," she said — not a question, a diagnosis, a small triumph. "That's good. That's *right*. You do that.
