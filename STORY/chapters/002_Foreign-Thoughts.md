# Chapter 2 — Foreign Thoughts

*from **A Nascent Light** — Vol. 1: First Light*

---

As the cascading colors faded from my vision, what I noticed first was the ceiling.

Not the white one. A *different* one: rough-hewn beams, a patch of dark that might have been a water stain and might have been a face, a rope of drying yarrow hanging where a smoke-hole should have been. I knew the shape of it in the dark because I had looked at it for five years. I just hadn't, until this morning, *known that I knew it.*

I didn't get up. I lay still and did what you do when something has gone wrong in a way you can't name: I took inventory. Hands — smaller than they should be, and rougher; the callus pattern of a child who starts work before he starts shaving. Legs — a half-head shorter than the ones I'd last owned. Chest — no scar where the night in the hospital had promised one. Lungs clear. The smell of pork fat and rye bread coming up from below the floor, and under it woodsmoke, sheep, cold, and something herbal I only half-recognized as my mother's hands.

*My mother.* The thought arrived attached to a woman's face I could picture in full: an oval jaw, grey eyes, a burn scar on the left forearm from the still. My mother. Not a metaphor for it either — the word had been *earned*, the way a debt is earned, over years of small accounts paid on time.

Foreign memories had been leaking into me for a while now. I could mark the start of it fairly precisely: the winter fever, six months back, the three days that got me the sickroom and the willow-bark tea and the feeling of being carried through a tunnel of hot gravel. Before that, the leak had been occasional — a name for a tool I didn't have, a word for a sound this language had two sloppier words for. After that, it ran. By this morning it had simply become me, the way water becomes a pond: no thunderous awakening, no heavens parting to bestow enlightenment. I had just, gradually and then all at once, remembered a life before this one.

Was I going crazy? I knew what the signs were; the old me — *Daniel*, the name felt like a coat that had been let out twice — had read enough to know how that story goes. But that had come on gradually in other people, over years, and this had the patient architecture of a memory, not the noise of a delusion. Delusions don't survive being cross-checked. I'd spent this spring cross-checking this one, quietly, while pretending to be eleven. It survived. I didn't know what that made me — heir to some dead man's residue, ghost with a heartbeat, or a boy whose soul had been wearing the wrong size his whole life and had finally shrunk the shirt.

I chose not to decide. Decisions without data are just anxiety wearing a hat.

"Kale!" Astrid's voice came through the floorboards, and then the actual stairs, and then my door. "Kale, you're going to let the fire go out and then *die* and it's going to be your fault."

"Good morning to you too," I called back, and the joke landed the way it always did — too old for my mouth, which was the entire reason for my weekly check-ins with the mirror.

I came down into the kitchen. "What to make, what to make," I muttered in the doorway, because Mother was already at the stove and Father was already at the table, and the inventory habit ran deep. There was nothing for it but to settle on eggs and toast; we didn't have the stuff to go all out, and there was no shame in that, because the shame is a city invention, invented by people who never had to count. I took the skillet off Mother's hands with a wordless turn of the wrist — mine was already hot, I knew — and cracked four eggs one-handed, because in the old life I'd learned it off a video and in this one I'd been practicing on everyone else's for two years.

"Someone's up with the sun," Father said, which was not true and would not be said twice. He was mending a snare, and he said things in the order their usefulness required. The bread toasted. The eggs went down. We ate like people who had been given a morning and knew it was a loan.

Here's the thing that most people misunderstand about a life like ours: it isn't hard the way stories insist frontier life is hard. The days were long and the work had an end. The sheep were a bank account with legs. The nearest neighbor was a mile of trail and half a generation away from caring about my opinion of him. My mother was the village midwife, which meant the whole village was a debt to her, and she ran the debt like a fund for the years the weather went bad. My father held timber-rights on two stands of the Greywood, which made us, in the arithmetic of Garrow's Ford, *comfortable* — a word people here only used after checking whether anyone could hear it.

It was, by any honest measure, a good hand. And hands like that are the ones you don't put down, because the alternative to holding them is finding out what you were when nobody needed you. I'd been that alternative once. I remembered exactly how it had felt to be useful to a server rack and beloved by nobody.

So here was my strategy, and I want it on the record — figuratively; actually putting anything in writing in a village that reads your chimney for news is how you end up as a rumor — my strategy was *usefulness*. Not heroics. Not prophecy. When I began making myself genuinely useful around the house and honest-to-goodness competent in the fields, the quality of my life improved dramatically, and no one ever once asked what had changed about me, because competence looks exactly like what a good son is supposed to become.

You have to understand the distinction the village was working from: they weren't simple. They were *specific*. Garrow's Ford had opinions the way the river had stones — settled by pressure, not stupidity. A boy who started whistling about other worlds got the herb-woman's pity and the pastor's attention, and either attention was an audit I could not survive. A boy who fixed the fence before being asked, remembered which ewe was lame, and could count the tally-sticks without his lips moving, got left the hell alone. I wasn't hiding, exactly. I was *curating*. Every one of my adult reflexes had to audition before it got to be seen.

The hardest part, as with most complex situations, was the eyes.

My mother noticed first. She always noticed — I think midwives are born with a tick-list behind their faces for the difference between *alive* and *alright*, and she'd been running it on me since the fever.

"Kale." She caught me at the door with the axe-head I'd taken to sharpen, and she turned my face up under her chin with two fingers, professional, unhurried, checking pupils I'd read about once in a book I didn't have. "You slept strange. You talked."

"Everyone talks with a fever's leftovers."

"You didn't have a fever this week." The words were kind. The precision under them wasn't the kind of kindness you get from people who make their living on the exact moment a birth stops being a miracle and starts being a delivery. "Look at me when I'm guessing."

I looked. "I dreamed about the howe," I said. It was the truest thing I could offer: I *had* dreamed about Greyfell Mound, most nights — the grass-grown shape of it up on the cold hill, the reason every shepherd in the valley made a wide arc at dusk and no one, not even Runa, would say what for. "Same one. The door with no door."

Her thumb pressed once, briefly, against the hinge of my jaw — the midwife's seal — and then she let me go. "Then eat something heavier before you go near an axe. Dreaming burns a hole in a boy."

"Yes ma'am," I said, and went to eat something heavier, and hated, a little, that being seen was starting to feel less like exposure and more like being *known*.

***

I let myself out to the woodshed under the flag of *sharpening things*, which was true, if incomplete. The real agenda was an hour with a blade, a block of ash, and my own hands, because a body is a claim that has to be filed daily.

The axe sang. The edge took the grain. And, methodically, the way you'd go through a tool-shed with a lantern, I went through the inventory of me.

What I knew: a life, twenty-eight years, mostly unremarkable, ending in a white room and an offer and a list of worlds and light coming through a hole in a wall. A system — I use the word loosely, because so far it had the courtesy to keep its hands to itself, having merely said *calibration complete* and *game start* in fonts I felt more than saw, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. I'd tried, in the spring, the cliched commands of the books Daniel had grown up on. "[Status]." "[Menu]." "[Inventory]?" Standing right where I was, in this light, with chips of ash at my feet, and I could repeat them here for old times' sake.

"Status."

Nothing. Of course nothing. I was a man — I'd been a man — talking to trees. What did you do, in that situation? You kept the ledger, you kept the faith that something was going on in the sub-basement of your skull while the ground floor looked busy, and you did the chores, because the sheep did not care about your metaphysics and either did I.

What I had — physically, measurably, before any magic got involved — was an eleven-year-old's frame with a decade of *intention* behind it. I ate more than was strictly normal for the size of me; I'd noticed that in the time it takes a second breakfast to look conspicuous, and written it off to growth, or fever-stock, or the small print of whatever was running down there. My reflexes were sharpening weekly. I could hold a thought — plan a knot, a snare-set, a sentence before I said it — longer and cleaner than I should have been able to, in the way that running a new program on old hardware still beats running a good one on ancient. Whatever the Kaleidoscope was doing to me, it wasn't *giving* me anything yet. It was *tuning* me. Waiting for something. I didn't know for what, and the not-knowing had stopped being frightening somewhere around the third month and quietly became a project. Projects I could do.

The other thing I knew was what Father knew, and this is where the axe work slowed: he'd promised the Ash Court's share of spring timber to the bailiff's factor by the last of the month, three stands of ash and a hundred rails, with two pairs of hands and one horse. That was the frontier for you — every contract in the world's back pockets, every year a fresh arithmetic of *can we*. We could. Barely. Like always, we could *barely*, and everyone called it a good hand.

I ran my thumb along the new edge of the axe and thought, very calmly, about ledgers.

*Useful*, I'd decided. *Stay useful.* There's a version of a strategy like that which is just fear wearing work boots, and I knew it, and I had made my peace with it, because I had also met the version of me that had spent his twenties being *useful* in a server room to people who never learned his name, and if my cover in this life cost me being quietly appreciated by people who would know my grave — fine. I'd take the trade. I was taking it *now*, actually, in a small way, every morning. The trade pays daily, and I spend it on hot food and a sister who is narrating a squirrel to itself through the fence.

"Kale! *Kale*, listen — it's got the nut in its cheek and it's *denying it*—"

I laughed out loud, an un-curated sound, and Astrid struck up like a match on the other side of the yard, holding a fistful of nothing and the absolute certainty of the eight-year-old.

"It's called storing," I told her. "They bury a hundred of them and forget half. The forest plants itself because the squirrel forgets where it put the change."

"That's stupid," she said, with reverence.

"That's *economics*," I said, and Father, from the far end of the shed, made a sound that was either disgust or a laugh.

---

I woke before dawn with a light in the corner of my eye that wasn't the hearth.

It hung there, patient as a watermark, translucent, and when I looked at it directly it shifted to the middle distance and resolved, and this time — *this time* — I did not imagine the little line of type that sat in it.

[Booting… 1%]

I lay in the dark in my small, tuned, useless, beloved body, and I smiled like an idiot with my face to the ceiling so nobody could see, and I whispered, to the room, to the room that had had a wall break down in front of me, "Well, took you long enough."

[Booting… 2%]

The voice from the white room said nothing. It didn't have to. The number was the whole message, and the number had *moved*, and I watched it glow in the dark like a promise with a payment schedule.

"Two percent," I whispered. "Alright." I closed my eyes, and I pulled the quilt up like a kid, which is exactly what I was, and it turned out that the part of me that had waited five years to stop cross-checking was the part that fell asleep mid-sentence. "You've got until I'm *twelve*— two percent a day — you've got until— I'm keeping the sheep warm till then, you hear me—"

I did not hear a reply. There was no reply. There was only the fire dying on schedule, the wind working the shutters the way wind works a thing it intends to respect eventually, and a number, patient behind my eyes.

[Booting… 3%]
