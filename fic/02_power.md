# Power — "The Beat 'Em Up"

Only the rules the story needs to stay consistent. No stats, no levels, no scores, no quests. The system is a machine: a terse box, no voice, no personality, no winking.

**How it works**
1. Once a week Eli may **name** one fictional character from any established IP. A card shows: the character, their world, what they're doing right now, what the win would copy. He may re-pick freely; the week's card is only a proposal until he accepts.
2. Accepting sends him into that character's universe, arriving within 200 m of them. Their world is **real and running** — he gets them as they *currently are*, not at whatever moment the story happened to show them. His franchise knowledge is a hypothesis, never a script.
3. **The target must be able to defend themselves on arrival** — not asleep, unconscious, incapacitated, or already mid-fight with someone else. No ambush wins.
4. Then he fights them. A fight is a combat contest, won when the other person can't continue, gives up, or is clearly beaten at terms they accepted. Self-defense counts: someone who attacks him can be beaten validly. Killing also "wins"; the system has no opinion — Eli does.
5. **Win = a full copy of the target's abilities:** their powers, their trained skills, their knowledge and languages, at the level they personally use them. It does not copy who they are — not their personality, not their raw mind, not their possessions, not their memories-as-life. He gets what they can *do*, not *what they are*.
6. **No time limit; home is frozen.** While he's in the host world, time passes there normally and stops everywhere else, including home. He can stay a night or a season; he returns to the same minute he left. He may abandon the challenge at will — one thought, instant, regardless of who's watching.
7. **Cadence:** after he returns (win, lose, or leave), the next challenge opens ~28 days later. The weekly card still shows him options in the meantime; he just can't enter with them.
8. **Nothing physical crosses the gate.** What he brings back is skill alone. In the host world he can earn, spend, equip, and buy freely — it stays there.
9. **One character, one challenge** — ever. But a world isn't sealed: a *different* target there is a new key. Rematches don't happen; second visits might.
10. **Safety valve:** an injury he couldn't survive untreated pulls him home at the last non-fatal moment. The box never comments on it.
11. Some names read **`unavailable`** with a one-line reason the size of a fortune cookie — including people who, like him, arrived carrying someone else's system. He doesn't know what it means. The story doesn't owe an explanation.
12. **Worm's locals are never forbidden — and he never tries.** Naming a classmate or a cape from his own city is a thought he refuses, not a rule he's given. If that ever changes, it changes because of who he's become by then.

**His selection philosophy (character logic — no numbers)**
He asks himself plain questions before naming anyone:
- Can I *realistically* win this? Not "win with plot armor" — win with the body, gear, and hours I actually have.
- What does the prize do for the life I actually have? (Staying alive, medicine, money, tools, options — not "cool.")
- Can I survive the world itself, as a nobody with no papers?
- Can I *prepare* there, or does the world punish a stranger on sight?
- Who gets hurt by me picking this — bystanders, the target, people who'd miss them?
- Is the reward worth the danger, *honestly*, or do I just want it?

Current biases, held *now* (and fair game for the story to challenge later): no main characters — protagonists are where his knowledge is worst and the plot lives; no kids; no fights that can't end with both people standing; prefer people who have a code or a rule he can learn, because a rule is a thing he can beat.
