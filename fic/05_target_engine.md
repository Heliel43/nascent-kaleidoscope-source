# THE TARGET ENGINE — how Eli picks (and how the story audits him)

**Canon instruction (from the author's directive, recorded as fiction):** "All powers don't have to be active, flashy. The first challenge you have to delicate. Look for where the first will be easy *and* worth it. Think like the mc." → **Eli's notebook, ch 2, verbatim on the page:**

> **RULES FOR THE FIRST ONE (write them down or you'll flinch):**
> 1. **Win probability, not vibes.** First fight is a scared kid vs. somebody's whole life. Only fight people I can *arrange* to beat. 85% or no card.
> 2. **Worth it, my way.** Package must work for the life I already have: staying alive in this city, the hospital skills, the hardware store, the bike, Mom's hours, Nora's rent. Not "cool." *Useful*. A power makes me a target; a craft makes me a shopkeeper.
> 3. **Survivable world.** I have no papers, no money, no power. The world has to be one where that's embarrassing, not fatal.
> 4. **A clean loss for them.** They can defend themselves (the rule), they're awake and armed-ish (the rule), they *consent to the bout* (mine), and losing must not ruin a life. I'm not stealing from someone's family.
> 5. **Exit.** If it goes wrong I say one word and I'm home. So: no places where being teleported out means someone I saw dies offscreen.
> 6. **No main characters.** Not yet. Rules 1–5 are for people, not protagonists. (Break this only when rules 1–5 aren't enough to save people here. That's *later*. That's the whole story.)

## The scoring sheet (agent-side; every candidate gets these six numbers before anyone says the name on the page)
`WIN%` × `VALUE (for Eli's life, not for the plot)` × `WORLD LETHALITY for a baseline` × `MORAL TOLL` × `SECRECY IMPACT in BB` × `EXIT LINE`. Any candidate failing a rule above is *rejected with the reasoning on the page* — the rejection scenes are half the fun and all of the characterization.
